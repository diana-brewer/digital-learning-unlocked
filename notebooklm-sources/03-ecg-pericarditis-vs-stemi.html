ECG Teaching Note — Acute Pericarditis vs. STEMI

ECG interpretation guide · APP Fellowship Curriculum · 2026

1. Why This Distinction Matters

Acute pericarditis produces ST-segment elevation — and in a busy urgent care or emergency department, "ST elevation" triggers a cascade: the STEMI protocol, cath-lab activation, and anticoagulation. For a healthy 19-year-old with viral pericarditis, that cascade leads to unnecessary invasive angiography, anticoagulation risk, and a missed simple diagnosis. Conversely, a territorial STEMI dismissed as "probably pericarditis" in an older adult is a fatal error. The ECG is the first and fastest discriminator, and APP fellows must read the pattern, not just "ST elevation = bad."

2. The Four Stages of Pericarditis on ECG

StageTimingFindings
I — AcuteFirst daysDiffuse concave (saddle-shaped) ST elevation in I, II, aVL, aVF, V2–V6; PR-segment depression in the same leads; reciprocal ST depression + PR elevation isolated to aVR. No pathologic Q waves.
II — Early repolarization phaseDays to ~2 weeksST segments return to baseline; T-wave amplitude decreases (flattening).
III — T-wave inversionWeeksT waves invert in the leads that previously showed ST elevation — can be mistaken for ischemic T-wave inversion.
IV — ResolutionWeeks to monthsT waves normalize. Up to 10–15% may have persistent nonspecific changes.

PR-segment depression, present in up to 80% of stage I cases, is the single most specific ECG finding for pericarditis — caused by atrial epicardial inflammation (atrial repolarization vector shift).

The aVR Sign — Quick Rule
When you see diffuse ST elevation, look at aVR: ST elevation in aVR + diffuse concave ST elevation + PR depression = pericarditis pattern. A single-territory convex ST elevation with reciprocal depression in a different territory (not just aVR) = STEMI pattern — activate. (One caution: ST elevation in aVR with diffuse ST depression elsewhere suggests left main or severe triple-vessel ischemia — an entirely different emergency.)

4. Pitfalls and Traps

  • Early repolarization (benign): concave ST elevation in V4–V5, most often in young athletic men, but no PR depression, no fever, no friction rub, and unchanged on prior ECGs. Comparison with an old ECG is decisive.
  • Localized pericarditis: pericarditis is occasionally focal (typically V3–V5) and can perfectly mimic anterior STEMI. Correlate clinically — history, rub, CRP, troponin, and echo; when in doubt, serial ECGs and cardiology consultation, not anticoagulation.
  • Myopericarditis: pericarditis pattern + elevated troponin. Risk stratify with echo; some of these patients have reduced EF or arrhythmias and need cardiology involvement and true activity restriction.
  • Dressler syndrome: weeks after MI or cardiac surgery — can look identical; get the history.
  • Spodick sign: downsloping TP segment — suggestive of pericarditis, subtle; a bonus finding for advanced readers.

5. Decision Framework for the APP

  1. See diffuse ST elevation? Run the pattern check: concave? PR depression? aVR sign? Pleuritic/positional pain? Recent URI? → pericarditis pathway.
  2. See a single territory of ST elevation? Especially convex, with reciprocal depression, Q waves, or hemodynamic instability → STEMI pathway, activate.
  3. Ambiguous? (focal changes, risk factors present, atypical story) → treat as ACS until excluded: serial ECGs, serial troponins, cardiology. Missing a STEMI is the error that cannot be undone.
  4. Diagnosed pericarditis? → NSAID + colchicine, echo for everyone, look for tamponade physiology, restrict activity, and normalize CRP before tapering (see accompanying clinical reference).
