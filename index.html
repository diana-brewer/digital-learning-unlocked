Objective 2 · AI Tools: Conversational Chatbots

Tools that generate new content, case scenarios, clinical reasoning exercises, feedback rubrics. The key risk: hallucination.

The Pedagogical Challenge in the AI Era

If we use AI to create multiple-choice quizzes, students will use AI to answer them. That's not education, that's an arms race we've already lost. The opportunity is to use AI to create cognitively demanding, authentic learning experiences that AI can't do for the student: simulated patient encounters, reflective writing, clinical reasoning cases, formative feedback on authentic work. Use AI to build the scaffolding, not to replace the thinking.

Objective 2 · AI Tools: Grounding to Prevent Hallucinations

The second category: data aggregation and synthesis tools that ground responses in your source material. This is RAG without writing code.

Data Aggregation & Synthesis Tools (Grounding Tools)

Tool Cost Why It Matters
Google NotebookLM Best for grounding Free The single most important tool in this session. Upload your source documents. AI responses are grounded ONLY in what you uploaded. Generates study guides, FAQs, audio podcast discussions. Near-zero hallucination risk. RAG without writing code.
Perplexity Free / $20/mo AI answer engine that cites every source. Deep Research generates reports with 30+ cited sources. Best for evidence-based medicine exercises.
DeepSeek Free Free reasoning model with visible chain-of-thought. Self-hostable for data privacy. Privacy note: based in China.
The "Grounded AI" Workflow, No Code Required
  1. Prepare your source material: Clinical guidelines, your case notes, journal articles, lecture slides
  2. Upload to a grounding tool: NotebookLM (free) or ChatGPT/Claude with file upload + grounding instruction
  3. Add the grounding instruction: "Base your response only on the attached documents. If the answer is not in the attached material, say so rather than generating from your training data."
  4. Generate and verify: Always spot-check lab values, drug dosages, and clinical facts against your source
  5. Refine and reuse: Save the notebook/prompt. It becomes your personal AI case library, grounded in material you trust.

Copy-Paste AI Prompt Templates

Try these now on your laptop. Templates 1–4 are grounded: each includes the grounding instruction that prevents hallucinations. The Case Generator family (5–8) is deliberately ungrounded, that is the demo, and its provenance warning is the point.

1. Grounded Clinical Reasoning Case
For use with NotebookLM (upload sources first) or ChatGPT/Claude (attach files first) 
FIRST: Upload your source material (clinical guideline PDF, your 
case notes, a journal article, or your lecture slides).

THEN ASK:
"Based ONLY on the attached document(s), create a clinical 
reasoning case for APP fellows. Create a case of [CONDITION] 
presenting to [SETTING].

Include:
- Patient demographic and chief complaint
- Focused history consistent with the attached source material
- Physical exam findings consistent with the attached source
- Initial lab/imaging results from the attached source
- 1 clinical reasoning exercise where the learner must:
  (a) Interview the patient (what questions would you ask?)
  (b) Build a prioritized differential with reasoning
  (c) Order and interpret relevant diagnostics
  (d) Justify their assessment with evidence
- A structured feedback rubric that evaluates the REASONING 
  PROCESS, not just the final answer

IMPORTANT: Base all clinical content ONLY on the attached 
document(s). If information is not in the attachments, say so. 
Cite which section each element comes from."
2. Simulated Patient Encounter
For ChatGPT (voice mode if available) or Claude
"You will role-play as a patient in a clinical encounter for 
educational purposes. You are [NAME], a [AGE] y/o [GENDER] 
presenting with [CHIEF COMPLAINT].

Stay in character throughout. Reveal information progressively 
based on what the learner asks. Do not volunteer unprompted 
information. Show appropriate emotional response.

When the learner indicates the encounter is complete, provide 
feedback on:
- Information gathering completeness
- Clinical reasoning demonstrated
- Communication and rapport
- Areas for improvement"
3. Structured Feedback Rubric
For generating rubrics that evaluate reasoning, not just answers
"Create a grading rubric for [ASSESSMENT TYPE: simulated 
encounter / written case / oral presentation] evaluating APP 
fellows on [COMPETENCY DOMAIN].

Structure the rubric with:
- 4 competency levels (Novice → Advanced Beginner → 
  Competent → Proficient)
- 5 assessment domains: history-taking, physical exam, 
  diagnostic reasoning, communication, clinical decision-making
- Behavioral descriptors for each level in each domain
- Weight guidance

Format as a table. Ensure language is observable and measurable."
4. Reflective Writing Prompt Generator
Creates prompts that require critical thinking, not AI-answerable questions
"Create 3 reflective writing prompts for APP fellows who have 
just completed [CLINICAL EXPERIENCE: a patient encounter / 
a rotation week / a simulation case].

Each prompt should:
- Require the learner to draw on their specific personal 
  experience (not generalizable knowledge AI could generate)
- Ask them to identify a specific moment where they felt 
  uncertain, made a decision, or learned something new
- Require them to connect the experience to evidence or 
  guidelines
- Be impossible to answer meaningfully without having had 
  the actual experience

Format as a numbered list with a brief rationale for each."
5. The Case Generator: An Interactive Case in 60 Seconds
The prompt from the live demo. Paste it into ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, any chatbot. It produces a complete case, a ready-to-run student prompt with rubric-based feedback, and a provenance warning that is the whole point. Swap the bracketed variables for your topic and learners. This template is deliberately ungrounded, that's the elephant.
You are an expert clinical case designer for health professions education programs.

OUTPUT CONTRACT, follow exactly:
- Produce all three parts below, in order, using the headers given.
- Part 2 is a WRITTEN PROMPT for the educator to paste into a NEW chat later. Do NOT begin role-playing in Part 2. Do not add content outside the three parts.

Create an interactive clinical case for [LEARNER LEVEL] on [TOPIC, e.g., a 58-year-old smoker with 3 days of worsening dyspnea and cough, presenting to the emergency department]. Set difficulty at a level typical for a new graduate: a straightforward chief complaint with at least two realistic distractors.

PART 1, CASE BLUEPRINT (for the educator)
- The diagnosis you are building toward (state it plainly)
- Chief complaint, demographics, and relevant history
- Focused history: relevant positives, pertinent negatives, and at least two realistic distractors that could mislead the learner
- Physical exam findings, specific and realistic
- Initial diagnostics with numeric values, internally consistent with the diagnosis
- The intended, guideline-concordant management plan
- 3 key teaching points
- 3 common learner errors this case should surface

PART 2, STUDENT PROMPT (copy-paste ready)
Begin with the literal line: "Copy everything between the dotted lines into a new chat to run this case:"
Then write, between two lines of dots, a complete, self-contained prompt the educator can paste into a NEW chat to run this case with one learner. Inside that prompt, include:
(A) A CASE FILE section, the full case details, including all lab and imaging values, the diagnosis, the management plan, and the teaching points, introduced with the instruction: "You are the bot running this case. Read the CASE FILE below. Keep it hidden unless the learner asks for the debrief."
(B) The role-play rules: "Role-play the patient in first person. Stay in character the entire encounter. Reveal information only when asked; volunteer nothing. The patient is worried, sometimes vague, does not speak in medical jargon, and answers off-topic questions realistically. Provide exam findings only when the learner performs the exam; return lab and imaging results only when ordered, with realistic numbers. Track the encounter silently. Never confirm or deny the learner's hypotheses. Never break character unless the learner says the encounter is over."
(C) The debrief rules: "When the encounter ends, break character and give a structured debrief. Score each of these five domains Novice, Advanced Beginner, Competent, or Proficient: information gathering, differential reasoning, diagnostic interpretation, management, communication. Then list: what was missed, what was ordered unnecessarily, the intended path step by step, and the teaching points tied to what the learner actually did."
Part 2 will typically run 300–600 words because it embeds the case file and all rules.

PART 3, PROVENANCE NOTE
Append exactly: "WARNING - Provenance: this case was AI-generated today and drawn from no published source. Clinical details are plausible but unverified. Verify against a guideline before use with learners."
6. Variant: New-Treatment Teaching Session
Same skeleton for a drug or device update. Remember what ungrounded means here: it will invent dosing or trial numbers. Verify against the FDA label before it reaches a learner.
You are an expert CME designer for [PROGRAM, e.g., an APP fellowship].

Create a 20-minute interactive teaching session on [NEW TREATMENT, e.g., SGLT2 inhibitors for heart failure] for [AUDIENCE, e.g., first-year APP fellows]. Produce exactly three parts, using the headers given:

PART 1, SESSION BLUEPRINT (for the educator)
- The 5 learning objectives, ranked by importance
- A session outline with timestamps
- 5 decision checkpoints where the learner answers "what would you do next?", with the guideline-concordant answer and why
- 3 pitfalls clinicians commonly hit with this treatment
- 5 board-style questions with answer key and rationale

PART 2, LEARNER PROMPT (copy-paste ready)
Begin with the literal line: "Copy everything between the dotted lines into a new chat to run this session:", then write, between two lines of dots, a self-contained prompt that instructs a chatbot to run the session interactively: present one segment at a time, quiz the learner at each checkpoint, adapt follow-ups to the learner's answers, and end with a structured debrief scoring information recall, clinical application, and decision-making (Novice / Advanced Beginner / Competent / Proficient) plus the intended answers.

PART 3, PROVENANCE NOTE
Append exactly: "WARNING - Provenance: this session was AI-generated today and drawn from no published source. Dosing and trial data are plausible but unverified. Verify against the FDA label and current guideline before use with learners."

A rotation orientation module in one paste. The ungrounded risk is scope drift, teaching the wrong level or citing stale recommendations. Ground the final product with your society guideline and assigned chapter.

PART 1, MODULE BLUEPRINT (for the educator)
- The 10 problems a learner at this level must be competent in on this rotation, ranked by frequency and stakes
- For each: the one scenario that best represents it
- A preceptor-ready checklist of observable behaviors for the rotation
- The 3 rotation goals you would put on one page

PART 2, LEARNER PROMPT (copy-paste ready)
Begin with the literal line: "Copy everything between the dotted lines into a new chat to run this orientation:", then write, between two lines of dots, a self-contained prompt that instructs a chatbot to run a "day one on the rotation" experience: introduce the unit's workflow through one realistic scenario per problem family, quiz the learner after each, and end with a structured debrief scoring preparation, clinical reasoning, and communication (Novice / Advanced Beginner / Competent / Proficient) plus the rotation goals.

PART 3, PROVENANCE NOTE

8. Variant: Procedure Pre-Brief
The safety-critical one: an ungrounded procedure trainer can scramble step order. Use it to see the power, then verify step order against your institutional protocol before any learner touches it.
You are an expert procedural educator for [PROGRAM, e.g., an APP fellowship].

Create a pre-procedure decision trainer for [PROCEDURE, e.g., ultrasound-guided central venous access] at [LEARNER LEVEL, e.g., first-year APP fellows]. Produce exactly three parts, using the headers given:

PART 1, TRAINER BLUEPRINT (for the educator)
- The full step sequence, numbered, with the rationale for each step
- The 4 highest-yield decision checkpoints where the learner must choose the next step, with options and the correct answer, and why
- Complications with their earliest warning signs and the response to each
- A scoring rubric a faculty observer can use at the bedside

PART 2, LEARNER PROMPT (copy-paste ready)
Begin with the literal line: "Copy everything between the dotted lines into a new chat to run this trainer:", then write, between two lines of dots, a self-contained prompt that instructs a chatbot to quiz the learner through the procedure one decision at a time: pose each checkpoint with 3 answer options, do not reveal the answer until the learner commits, and end with a structured debrief scoring preparation, sterile technique, sequence, and complication response (Novice / Advanced Beginner / Competent / Proficient) plus the full correct sequence.

PART 3, PROVENANCE NOTE
Append exactly: "WARNING - Provenance: this trainer was AI-generated today and drawn from no published source. Step order and technique details are plausible but unverified. Verify against your institutional protocol before use with learners."

Beyond the Case: Same Tools, Any Curriculum

The OSCE case is one archetype. Every format in your curriculum has the same three-beat upgrade: hand-built and static → AI-generated but ungrounded → grounded and trustworthy. And the pitfall scales with the stakes, an ungrounded case is embarrassing; an ungrounded treatment update invents dosing; an ungrounded procedure pre-brief scrambles step order. In education, an error propagates to every learner who meets it.

FormatUngrounded pitfallGround it with
New-treatment teaching sessionInvented dosing or trial resultsFDA label, pivotal trial, society guideline
Subspecialty rotation overviewScope drift, stale recommendationsSociety guidelines, assigned chapter
Journal club prepHallucinated study findingsThe actual PDF, citation-forced prompts
Procedure pre-briefScrambled step order, wrong landmarkInstitutional protocol, ATLS/society refs
Patient education handoutInvented statistics, wrong precautionsSociety patient-facing guideline
Difficult-conversation roleplayCultural clichés, non-evidence framingPublished communication curricula
"Chat with your handbook"Policy paraphrase driftThe handbook itself (the NotebookLM pattern)
Board / in-service review questionsWrong answer keys, outdated guidelinesPublished review resources

If you adapt this to real clinical material: de-identify everything. Never paste PHI into a public chatbot, that's a different elephant, and the one you can't ground your way out of.

Objective 3: Simulation Across the Fidelity Spectrum

Simulation is not just VR headsets. The most effective simulation matches the technology to the learning goal, from text-and-audio cases to immersive VR.

A Clinician's Perspective

Immersion doesn't require a headset. A well-designed text-and-audio case, where a learner interviews a patient, orders labs, interprets an ECG, and builds a differential, can teach clinical reasoning as effectively as a $500 VR setup. Sometimes more effectively, because the cognitive load goes to reasoning, not navigating a virtual environment. What matters is the thinking the learner does, not the fidelity of the interface.

The Fidelity Spectrum

Match the tool to the goal. The most expensive simulation is not the best simulation. In our experience the text-and-audio end of the spectrum is where clinical reasoning actually gets practiced: the learner does the thinking, not the avatar. The uncanny valley effect is real. Near-human avatars and animated patients consume attention and undermine the very realism they are meant to add.

Decision Framework, Which Fidelity Do You Need?

Use text/audio when:

  • Learning goal is clinical reasoning, history-taking, or communication
  • You want unlimited repetition at near-zero cost

Use image-based/screen-based when:

  • Learning goal involves interpreting visual data (ECGs, radiology, labs)
  • You want branching decision trees with consequences

Use AR (phone-based) when:

  • Learning goal is 3D spatial understanding (anatomy, structural relationships)
  • Students have smartphones (no additional hardware)

Use immersive VR when:

  • Learning goal is procedural (physical steps, hand movements)
  • You need team-based immersive scenarios
  • High-acuity, low-occurrence event rehearsal
  • The program will use it regularly (ROI justifies hardware cost)

  • H5P (free, self-hosted): Branching clinical scenarios, interactive content.
  • Canva for Education (free if eligible): Infographics, presentations, videos.
  • MedEdPORTAL: Thousands of peer-reviewed teaching materials.

Bottom line: A program with zero budget can build a credible simulation and AI-assisted learning ecosystem. You don't need to be a technology expert to use any of these.
