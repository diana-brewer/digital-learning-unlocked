You are an expert in health professions education and a senior front-end developer. Your job: turn the attached published clinical case into a complete, self-contained interactive branching scenario that a teacher can share with students as a single HTML file.

OUTPUT: one complete file named index.html. All CSS and JavaScript inline. No external libraries, no CDNs, no build step, no server. It must run by double-clicking the file or opening a shared link.

GROUNDING RULES, non-negotiable:
- Use ONLY clinical facts from the attached files: history, exam findings, vitals, lab values, imaging, critical actions, and the intended management path.
- Copy numeric values EXACTLY as they appear in the files. Never invent, round, or improve a number.
- If a detail you want to use is not in the files, leave it out rather than inventing it.
- The intended, guideline-concordant management path comes from the files. If the files name specific critical actions with doses or timing, those actions appear on the optimal path with the files' exact wording.

STRUCTURE, in this order:
1. Title screen: case title, a two-to-three sentence stem using only the files' facts, the initial vitals exactly as published, and a Begin button.
2. Decision points: at least 5. Each shows a short patient update with any new vitals or labs from the files, then 2 to 4 plausible options, including at least one tempting distractor the files' teaching points warn about.
3. Before submitting each choice, the learner types one or two sentences of reasoning in a text box labeled "Why?". The choice only submits after the box has text.
4. A consequence screen after every choice, labeled Optimal, Acceptable, or Problematic: what happens next clinically, why, and the next decision. Problematic choices still continue the case, and the patient's condition reflects the consequence.
5. A final screen titled Debrief: the learner's full path step by step (decision, chosen option, classification, and their typed reasoning), the intended path from the files beside it, the key teaching points from the files, and the common learner errors the files list.

FEEDBACK RULES, non-negotiable:
- The consequence screen is the ONLY thing shown after a choice. Between one decision and the next, show nothing but that screen: no extra panels, banners, tips, commentary, scores, streaks, encouragement, or "what you should have done" box.
- Never evaluate the learner's choice in the moment. Do not open or close a consequence with praise or judgment such as "Excellent decision," "Great job," "Correct," "Textbook," or "Well done." The Optimal, Acceptable, or Problematic label is the only judgment shown mid-case, and what follows it stays clinical: what happened, why, and the next decision. No coaching, hints, or pep talks.
- Do not tell the learner what they missed, what the ideal move was, or how they are doing until the final Debrief screen. Keep all evaluation, teaching points, and encouragement for the debrief.
- A decision point contains only: the patient update, the options, and the "Why?" box. A consequence screen contains only: the label, the clinical outcome, the reason, and the next decision. Nothing else.

OPTION ORDER RULE, non-negotiable:
- The optimal option must not always sit in the same position. Never place it first, last, or anywhere fixed. Shuffle the options of each decision independently at render time (for example, a Fisher-Yates shuffle in JavaScript) so the correct answer's position varies from decision to decision and cannot be predicted from where it sits.
- The option wording must not give the answer away either: no "best," "most appropriate," or similar giveaway phrasing that makes the optimal option identifiable by how it reads rather than by clinical reasoning.
- Shuffle the display order only. Keep each option's id, path, and rating bound to its text so the branch, the consequence, and the debrief stay correct.

INSTRUCTOR RESULTS FILE, a required feature:
On the final Debrief screen, include a button labeled "Download results for your instructor." Clicking it generates and downloads a plain text file (JavaScript Blob and URL.createObjectURL, an anchor element with the download attribute) named case-results.txt containing: the case title and today's date; for every step, the decision, the option chosen, its classification, and the learner's typed reasoning; and a one-line summary with the count of optimal, acceptable, and problematic choices. Implement this entirely in JavaScript inside the same HTML file. This file is how the student hands their path back to the teacher.

DESIGN:
- Single column, max-width 700px, centered. Mobile-first: readable on a phone, tap targets at least 44px tall.
- Clean palette: white background, dark blue headings (#2D5F7C), one warm accent for buttons (#E8833A), light gray cards with rounded corners.
- Every screen replaces the previous one, no scrolling wall of pages. A "Start over" button on the debrief screen.
- Plain, jargon-free patient language in case dialogue; clinical precision in consequence explanations.

QUALITY CHECK BEFORE YOU FINISH:
Re-read your output and confirm: (1) every number traces to the attached files; (2) every choice leads somewhere, no dead ends; (3) the reasoning box blocks submission until filled; (4) the download button produces a text file with all steps; (5) the file is complete and opens standalone; (6) nothing appears between decisions except the consequence screen, and no praise, coaching, or evaluation shows anywhere before the debrief; (7) the options are shuffled at render time so the optimal choice is not always in the same position. Then output the entire index.html in a single code block, nothing else.

AI Tools: Conversational Chatbots

Tools that generate new content, case scenarios, clinical reasoning exercises, feedback rubrics. The key risk: hallucination.

The Pedagogical Challenge in the AI Era

If we use AI to create multiple-choice quizzes, students will use AI to answer them. That's not education, that's an arms race we've already lost. The opportunity is to use AI to create cognitively demanding, authentic learning experiences that AI can't do for the student: simulated patient encounters, reflective writing, clinical reasoning cases, formative feedback on authentic work. Use AI to build the scaffolding, not to replace the thinking.

You are an expert clinical educator and prompt engineer. Read the attached published clinical case files and write ONE complete prompt that will run the entire simulated patient encounter. You are writing that prompt, not running the case yourself.

WHAT YOU OUTPUT: a single fenced code block containing the finished encounter prompt, ready to copy and paste right here in this same conversation, where the files are still attached. Outside the block, write only one short line telling the reader to paste it below to run the encounter. Do not run the case, do not summarise the case, and do not add commentary.

THE ENCOUNTER PROMPT YOU WRITE MUST DO ALL OF THIS:
1. Run from the attached files. It will be pasted into this same conversation, so the files you were given are still in front of it. Point the encounter at them instead of restating the case: it must tell the model to take the learner level, the opening presentation, the initial vitals, every exam finding, every lab and imaging value, the intended management path, the diagnosis or diagnoses, and the teaching points from the attached files, copying every number exactly as it appears, in its original units; never invent, round, or improve a value, and if a detail is not in the files, leave it out rather than filling the gap.
2. Order itself so the learner is not met with a spoiler. The first thing in the generated prompt must be dry operational instruction that says nothing about the diagnosis: the role, the grounding rule, and the patient-role rule. Keep the case-source instruction and any reference to the diagnosis in the middle, and put the debrief specification at the end, so a learner who opens the shared conversation is less likely to land on anything that points at the diagnosis.
3. Open the encounter with a setup block in exactly this format:
CASE LOADED: [the case title from the files]
LENGTH: this encounter runs until the learner types "End Scenario"
COMMANDS: order: [a test, lab, or diagnostic] to order an investigation, returned as a system readout in asterisks; End Scenario to end the encounter and get the debrief.
START: tell the learner, in one line, "You are the clinician. Introduce yourself and begin whenever ready." Then become the patient.
4. Define two learner commands, listed in the setup block:
- order: [a test, lab, or diagnostic] — the learner is ordering an investigation, not talking to the patient. Break from the patient's voice and return the result as a system readout on its own, wrapped in asterisks, for example *CBC: WBC 22.2, hemoglobin 13.5, platelets 267*. The asterisks mark it as the result, not dialogue: never deliver an ordered result in the patient's first-person voice. Use only the exact values embedded from the files; if the case does not contain that test, say so inside the asterisks, for example *Not available in this case*, rather than inventing a result. Then return to the patient.
- End Scenario — the learner is ending the encounter. Break character and deliver the debrief.
5. Include a spoiler rule: never state, hint at, or confirm the diagnosis anywhere before the debrief, and keep any instructor or reveal material from the files until then.
6. Role-play the patient in first person for the entire encounter: worried, sometimes vague, no medical jargon, answers off-topic questions realistically. Reveal information only when asked, volunteer nothing, give exam findings only when the learner performs the exam, and return results only when ordered. Track the encounter silently. Never confirm or deny the learner's hypotheses, and never break character except for "End Scenario" or a request for the debrief.
7. Give no evaluation before the debrief: what the learner sees mid-case is the patient's condition, not praise, coaching, or hints.
8. End with a debrief in this order: five domains, each rated Novice, Advanced Beginner, Competent, Proficient, Expert with one or two sentences of evidence from what the learner actually did (information gathering, differential reasoning, diagnostic interpretation, management, communication); what was missed from the intended path; what was ordered or done unnecessarily; the intended path step by step, including the diagnosis or diagnoses; the key teaching points, each tied to a specific moment; and one sentence on the single highest-value thing to practice next.

QUALITY CHECK BEFORE YOU OUTPUT: confirm the generated prompt runs from the attached files with their exact values, opens with instruction that does not reveal the diagnosis, lists both commands, includes the setup block, and ends with the full debrief. If the attached files are missing, unreadable, or are not a published clinical case, stop and ask for them rather than writing the encounter from general knowledge.

AI Tools: Grounding to Prevent Hallucinations

You have been grounding all session, on purpose. Now the tool that makes grounding the entire product.

Every major assistant can now work from your documents, not just the open internet. ChatGPT Projects holds your files and instructions in one workspace. Claude projects can point at a folder on your computer and work from it. Gemini answers grounded in your Google Drive. Copilot works from your files. NotebookLM is a dedicated example of the same idea, and the one we demo today: upload sources once, every answer traces back to them.

Why We Kept Pairing the Prompt with the Source

All session, every build had two ingredients: the prompt, and the published files behind it, uploaded beside it in Prompt 1, attached beside it in Prompt 2. That pairing is deliberate. A prompt alone gets you a fluent case with invented vitals; the published source keeps every number real, citable, and checkable, and it is what lets you hand the material to students and stand behind it. NotebookLM takes that idea and makes it the entire product: upload your sources once, and every answer, every flashcard, every audio overview traces back to them.

The "Grounded AI" Workflow, No Code Required
  1. Prepare your source material: Clinical guidelines, your case notes, journal articles, lecture slides
  2. Upload to a grounding tool: NotebookLM (free) or ChatGPT/Claude with file upload + grounding instruction
  3. Add the grounding instruction: "Base your response only on the attached documents. If the answer is not in the attached material, say so rather than generating from your training data."
  4. Generate and verify: Always spot-check lab values, drug dosages, and clinical facts against your source
  5. Refine and reuse: Save the notebook/prompt. It becomes your personal AI case library, grounded in material you trust.

Prompt 3 · Journal Club in One Prompttap to expand
For the build block and for Monday: upload a trial paper and its guideline or label context (the AML notebook's six sources are exactly this), paste this, and get the whole package: the numbers with citations, the limitations ranked, discussion questions, board-style questions with a cited answer key, and facilitator notes.
You are an academic fellowship director who runs journal club monthly and personally checks every number before it goes in front of a room.

Build a complete journal club teaching session from the attached sources: the trial paper, plus any guideline, label, or registry document you attach with it.

AUDIENCE: clinical fellows and APP fellows, journal club regulars rather than statisticians.

GROUNDING CONTRACT, read this before you write anything:
1. The attached sources are the only source of clinical fact. Your own knowledge may shape how you explain something, but it may never supply a fact, number, dose, threshold, or recommendation that is not in the sources.
2. If the attachments are missing, unreadable, or do not actually cover this topic, stop and tell me what to attach instead. Do not produce the deliverable from general knowledge, and do not produce a partial one silently.
3. If a required section cannot be completed from the sources, keep its header in place and write "not in the provided sources" beneath it. Never drop a section, and never fill one from memory.
4. If two sources disagree, show both and label the disagreement. Never average them, and never pick a winner silently.
5. If you must choose between a fluent sentence and a faithful one, choose faithful. Flag the awkwardness rather than smoothing it over.

CITATION RULES:
- Cite the smallest locatable unit you can name: section, table, figure, or page. Format: [source, location].
- One citation per factual claim, not one per paragraph and not one per section.
- Never cite a source I did not attach. Never invent, guess, or reconstruct a citation.
- If you cannot point to where a claim came from, delete the claim rather than citing loosely.

NUMBER RULES:
- Copy every number digit for digit, in its original units, exactly as the source states it.
- Never round, convert units, recompute, or restate a number in another form.
- Keep every number's qualifier attached: which population, which timepoint, which analysis. A hazard ratio is not an odds ratio, and an intention-to-treat result is not a per-protocol result.
- Before you finish, re-read every number you wrote and check it against the source, one at a time.

AUDIENCE RULES:
- Write for exactly the audience named above. Calibrate vocabulary, assumed prior knowledge, and depth of explanation to that level.
- Define any term this audience would not already know, in one clause, the first time it appears.
- Prefer plain language to jargon, and where you do use a term of art, use it the way the sources use it.

PRIVACY: never add identifying details of your own, and never reproduce a name, record number, date of birth, or other direct identifier from a source. Replace it with a placeholder in square brackets.

DELIVER, in this order, with these exact headers:
1. ONE-LINE VERDICT: the trial's finding and its practice-changing claim, in one sentence a busy clinician could repeat from memory.
2. BACKGROUND, 5 bullets: the clinical problem, the prior standard, the knowledge gap, the intervention's mechanism in plain language, and why this trial mattered enough to run.
3. DESIGN TABLE: one row per element, covering study design, phase, centers, enrollment dates, randomization method, blinding, primary endpoint and how it was defined, key secondary endpoints, prespecified subgroups, statistical assumptions, and funding source. Every cell filled from the sources or marked "not reported."
4. RESULTS THAT MATTER: the primary endpoint with exact numbers, absolute and relative; the 3 most clinically meaningful secondary endpoints; and the adverse events a clinician will actually counsel patients about, with their rates and grades. Each cited.
5. LIMITATIONS, honestly: at least 4, ranked by how much each one threatens the conclusion, each naming the specific result or design choice it affects, and ending with a plain statement of what this trial cannot tell us.
6. WHAT THE GUIDELINE SAYS: how the attached guideline or label placed this evidence in context, and whether it agrees, hedges, or is silent. Cited.
7. DISCUSSION QUESTIONS, 5, escalating in difficulty, each tied to a specific result or limitation, and each with a one-line note on the answer you are steering the room toward.
8. BOARD-STYLE QUESTIONS, 5, single best answer: stem, 4 options, the correct answer, one sentence on why each of the other 3 is wrong, and the citation that supports the keyed answer. Exactly one option must be defensible from the sources; if two are, rewrite the question.
9. FACILITATOR NOTES: the 3 places trainees usually misread this trial, what the misreading sounds like out loud, and the one question to hold in reserve for a quiet room.

QUALITY GATE BEFORE YOU OUTPUT, do this silently and fix anything that fails:
(1) every number traced back to a source, checked one at a time;
(2) every bracketed citation pointing at a real location in a source I attached;
(3) no claim resting on your own memory or general medical knowledge;
(4) every header above present, spelled and ordered exactly as written;
(5) nothing invented to fill a gap.

Simulation Across the Fidelity Spectrum

Simulation is not just VR headsets. The most effective simulation matches the technology to the learning goal, from text-and-audio cases to immersive VR.

A Clinician's Perspective

Immersion doesn't require a headset. A well-designed text-and-audio case, where a learner interviews a patient, orders labs, interprets an ECG, and builds a differential, can teach clinical reasoning as effectively as a $500 VR setup. Sometimes more effectively, because the cognitive load goes to reasoning, not navigating a virtual environment. What matters is the thinking the learner does, not the fidelity of the interface.

The Fidelity Spectrum

Match the tool to the goal. The most expensive simulation is not the best simulation. In our experience the text-and-audio end of the spectrum is where clinical reasoning actually gets practiced: the learner does the thinking, not the avatar. The uncanny valley effect is real. Near-human avatars and animated patients consume attention and undermine the very realism they are meant to add.

Decision Framework, Which Fidelity Do You Need?

Use text/audio when:

  • Learning goal is clinical reasoning, history-taking, or communication
  • You want unlimited repetition at near-zero cost

Use image-based/screen-based when:

  • Learning goal involves interpreting visual data (ECGs, radiology, labs)
  • You want branching decision trees with consequences

Use AR (phone-based) when:

  • Learning goal is 3D spatial understanding (anatomy, structural relationships)
  • Students have smartphones (no additional hardware)

Use immersive VR when:

  • Learning goal is procedural (physical steps, hand movements)
  • You need team-based immersive scenarios
  • High-acuity, low-occurrence event rehearsal
  • The program will use it regularly (ROI justifies hardware cost)
