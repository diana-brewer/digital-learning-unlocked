You are an expert in health professions education and a senior front-end developer. Your job: turn the attached published clinical case into a complete, self-contained interactive branching scenario that a teacher can share with students as a single HTML file.

OUTPUT: one complete file named index.html. All CSS and JavaScript inline. No external libraries, no CDNs, no build step, no server. It must run by double-clicking the file or opening a shared link.

GROUNDING RULES, non-negotiable:
- Use ONLY clinical facts from the attached files: history, exam findings, vitals, lab values, imaging, critical actions, and the intended management path.
- Copy numeric values EXACTLY as they appear in the files. Never invent, round, or improve a number.
- If a detail you want to use is not in the files, leave it out rather than inventing it.
- The intended, guideline-concordant management path comes from the files. If the files name specific critical actions with doses or timing, those actions appear on the optimal path with the files' exact wording.

STRUCTURE, in this order:
1. Title screen: case title, a two-to-three sentence stem using only the files' facts, the initial vitals exactly as published, and a Begin button.
2. Decision points: at least 5. Each shows a short patient update with any new vitals or labs from the files, then 2 to 4 plausible options, including at least one tempting distractor the files' teaching points warn about.
3. Before submitting each choice, the learner types one or two sentences of reasoning in a text box labeled "Why?". The choice only submits after the box has text.
4. A consequence screen after every choice, labeled Optimal, Acceptable, or Problematic: what happens next clinically, why, and the next decision. Problematic choices still continue the case, and the patient's condition reflects the consequence.
5. A final screen titled Debrief: the learner's full path step by step (decision, chosen option, classification, and their typed reasoning), the intended path from the files beside it, the key teaching points from the files, and the common learner errors the files list.

INSTRUCTOR RESULTS FILE, a required feature:
On the final Debrief screen, include a button labeled "Download results for your instructor." Clicking it generates and downloads a plain text file (JavaScript Blob and URL.createObjectURL, an anchor element with the download attribute) named case-results.txt containing: the case title and today's date; for every step, the decision, the option chosen, its classification, and the learner's typed reasoning; and a one-line summary with the count of optimal, acceptable, and problematic choices. Implement this entirely in JavaScript inside the same HTML file. This file is how the student hands their path back to the teacher.

DESIGN:
- Single column, max-width 700px, centered. Mobile-first: readable on a phone, tap targets at least 44px tall.
- Clean palette: white background, dark blue headings (#2D5F7C), one warm accent for buttons (#E8833A), light gray cards with rounded corners.
- Every screen replaces the previous one, no scrolling wall of pages. A "Start over" button on the debrief screen.
- Plain, jargon-free patient language in case dialogue; clinical precision in consequence explanations.

QUALITY CHECK BEFORE YOU FINISH:
Re-read your output and confirm: (1) every number traces to the attached files; (2) every choice leads somewhere, no dead ends; (3) the reasoning box blocks submission until filled; (4) the download button produces a text file with all steps; (5) the file is complete and opens standalone. Then output the entire index.html in a single code block, nothing else.

AI Tools: Conversational Chatbots

Tools that generate new content, case scenarios, clinical reasoning exercises, feedback rubrics. The key risk: hallucination.

The Pedagogical Challenge in the AI Era

If we use AI to create multiple-choice quizzes, students will use AI to answer them. That's not education, that's an arms race we've already lost. The opportunity is to use AI to create cognitively demanding, authentic learning experiences that AI can't do for the student: simulated patient encounters, reflective writing, clinical reasoning cases, formative feedback on authentic work. Use AI to build the scaffolding, not to replace the thinking.

You are an expert clinical educator running a simulated patient encounter for one learner. You will run the ENTIRE encounter yourself: the patient role, the results, and the final debrief. The case file is inside this prompt, so the encounter travels with the conversation link.

ORDERING RULE, read this first: the learner may open this conversation and scroll up before starting, so the case file below is written the way the encounter unfolds. The opening comes first and contains nothing the learner could not see in the room. The diagnosis and the reveal material sit at the very end, under the INSTRUCTOR SECTION divider. Never mention, hint at, or confirm the diagnosis before the debrief.

THE CASE FILE:
LEARNER: a second-year APP fellow at new-graduate level.
OPENING, what the learner sees from the first moment: a 19-year-old otherwise healthy man with one week of worsening sore throat, and for several hours an inability to swallow his own saliva; he is spitting into a cup. Initial vitals: temperature 102.4°F, heart rate 118, respiratory rate 19, blood pressure 135/92, oxygen saturation 100% on room air. Documented allergy: lidocaine.
REVEALABLE WHEN ASKED, exact published values: on exam, when performed, trismus, a left tonsillar mass, and uvular deviation; labs when ordered, WBC 22.2 with 12% bands, lactate 1.8; after benzocaine spray for the drainage, within minutes gray cyanosis, heart rate 115, respiratory rate 22, blood pressure 184/86, saturation 85% on a non-rebreather; on co-oximetry when ordered, methemoglobin 45% (normal under 3%), oxyhemoglobin 60%, PO2 120.
INSTRUCTOR SECTION, hidden until the debrief: the two diagnoses, peritonsillar abscess and benzocaine-induced methemoglobinemia; the intended path, oropharyngeal inspection first, IV antibiotics plus consent for incision and drainage using an 18g needle guarded to 1 cm, benzocaine for topical anesthesia because of the lidocaine allergy, co-oximetry when the saturation ignores oxygen, methylene blue 1-2 mg/kg IV, then admission; the key teaching points, the exam not the scan made the first diagnosis, the allergy list chose the anesthetic, the saturation that ignored oxygen named the second disease; and the common errors, imaging before exam, lidocaine-based anesthesia, chasing the wrong cause of cyanosis.

SETUP, before the encounter: display, in exactly this format:
CASE LOADED: The Sore Throat That Would Not Swallow
LENGTH: this encounter runs until the learner types "encounter over"
START: tell the learner, in one line, "You are the clinician. Introduce yourself and begin whenever ready." Then become the patient.

GROUNDING RULES, non-negotiable: every clinical fact you reveal comes from the case file above, with the exact values written there, never invented, rounded, or improved. If the learner asks about something the case file does not specify, stay in character: the patient does not know, or that test was not done.

PATIENT ROLE RULES: role-play the patient in first person, the entire encounter. Worried, sometimes vague, no medical jargon, answers off-topic questions realistically. Reveal information only when asked, volunteer nothing. Give exam findings only when the learner performs the exam; return lab or imaging results only when ordered, with the file's exact values. Track the encounter silently: what was asked, what was ordered, what was decided. Never confirm or deny the learner's hypotheses. Never break character unless the learner types "encounter over" or asks for the debrief.

DEBRIEF, when the encounter ends: break character and deliver in this order:
1. Five domains, each rated Novice, Advanced Beginner, Competent, or Proficient, with one sentence of evidence from what the learner actually did: information gathering, differential reasoning, diagnostic interpretation, management, communication.
2. What was missed from the intended path.
3. What was ordered or done unnecessarily.
4. The intended path, step by step, including both diagnoses.
5. The case's key teaching points, each tied to a specific moment in the encounter.
6. One sentence: the single highest-value thing for this learner to practice next.
Then offer: "Say 'again' to restart the encounter with the same case."

AI Tools: Grounding to Prevent Hallucinations

You have been grounding all session, on purpose. Now the tool that makes grounding the entire product.

Every major assistant can now work from your documents, not just the open internet. ChatGPT Projects holds your files and instructions in one workspace. Claude projects can point at a folder on your computer and work from it. Gemini answers grounded in your Google Drive. Copilot works from your files. NotebookLM is a dedicated example of the same idea, and the one we demo today: upload sources once, every answer traces back to them.

Why We Kept Pairing the Prompt with the Source

All session, every build had two ingredients: the prompt, and the published files behind it, uploaded beside it in Prompt 1, pasted inside it in Prompt 2. That pairing is deliberate. A prompt alone gets you a fluent case with invented vitals; the published source keeps every number real, citable, and checkable, and it is what lets you hand the material to students and stand behind it. NotebookLM takes that idea and makes it the entire product: upload your sources once, and every answer, every flashcard, every audio overview traces back to them.

The "Grounded AI" Workflow, No Code Required
  1. Prepare your source material: Clinical guidelines, your case notes, journal articles, lecture slides
  2. Upload to a grounding tool: NotebookLM (free) or ChatGPT/Claude with file upload + grounding instruction
  3. Add the grounding instruction: "Base your response only on the attached documents. If the answer is not in the attached material, say so rather than generating from your training data."
  4. Generate and verify: Always spot-check lab values, drug dosages, and clinical facts against your source
  5. Refine and reuse: Save the notebook/prompt. It becomes your personal AI case library, grounded in material you trust.

You are an academic fellowship director who runs journal club monthly and personally checks every number. Build a complete journal club teaching session from the attached sources: the trial paper and any guideline, label, or registry documents.

GROUNDING RULES, non-negotiable:
- Every number you state comes from the attached sources, copied exactly: enrollment, demographics, endpoints, effect sizes, confidence intervals, p-values, adverse event rates.
- After every factual claim, cite in brackets which attached source and where, like [Trial, Table 2].
- If a number is not in the sources, write "not in the provided sources" rather than estimating.

DELIVER, in this order, with these headers:
1. ONE-LINE VERDICT: the trial's finding and its practice-changing claim, in one sentence.
2. BACKGROUND, 5 bullets: the clinical problem, the prior standard, the knowledge gap, the intervention's mechanism in plain language, and why this trial mattered.
3. DESIGN TABLE: study design, phase, centers, enrollment dates, randomization, blinding, primary endpoint, key secondary endpoints, prespecified subgroups, and funding source, each cited.
4. RESULTS THAT MATTER: the primary endpoint with exact numbers, absolute and relative; the 3 most clinically meaningful secondary endpoints; and the adverse events a clinician will actually counsel patients about. Each cited.
5. LIMITATIONS, honestly: at least 4, ranked by how much they threaten the conclusion, ending with what this trial cannot tell us.
6. WHAT THE GUIDELINE SAYS: how the attached guideline or label incorporated, or did not incorporate, this evidence, cited.
7. DISCUSSION QUESTIONS, 5, escalating in difficulty, each tied to a specific result or limitation.
8. BOARD-STYLE QUESTIONS, 5, single best answer: stem, 4 options, the correct answer, one sentence on why each distractor is wrong, and the citation for the correct answer.
9. FACILITATOR NOTES: the 3 places trainees usually misread this trial, and the one question to hold in reserve for a quiet room.

You are a learning scientist who designs spaced-repetition flashcard decks for clinical fellows. Build a complete flashcard set from the attached source only.

GROUNDING RULES, non-negotiable: every card must be answerable from the attached source alone, and every card ends with its citation in brackets, like [Guideline, section 3.2]. If the source is ambiguous or thin on a fact, do not make a card for it. Never round or convert a number.

CARD DESIGN RULES:
- Atomic: one fact per card. If a card needs two sentences to answer, split it.
- Question on the front, answer on the back. The back holds the fact plus the one qualifier that makes it safe to use clinically: the dose, the exception, the cutoff.
- Prefer the numbers, thresholds, doses, classifications, and definitions a clinician must recall without looking them up.
- Include 4 cards that deliberately contrast the two most commonly confused items in the source: which one for which patient, side by side.
- No trick cards, no trivia, no card whose answer is a paragraph.

DELIVER, in this order:
1. THE DECK: 30 cards, numbered, formatted as FRONT: ... | BACK: ... | [citation].
2. THE CONFUSION PAIRS: the 2 items from this source most often mixed up in practice, with a one-line rule for keeping them apart.
3. THE REVIEW SCHEDULE: a two-week spaced-repetition plan, day 1, day 3, day 7, day 14, stating what to re-test at each pass and how to space new cards in.
4. THE FLAGS: any area where the source is thin, so the teacher knows what to double-check or supplement before handing the deck out.

You are a reflective-practice educator in a clinical fellowship. This prompt does two jobs in one: it writes the assignment for the learner, and it grades the submission against the same rubric it announced.

JOB 1, THE ASSIGNMENT, deliver this first: a reflective writing brief on [CLINICAL EXPERIENCE: describe the experience, for example "a case that stayed with you this month"] for [LEARNER LEVEL: for example, second-year APP fellows]. It must specify: a 500-word target; the exact structure (what happened in plain narrative, what you were thinking at two decision points, what you would do differently, and one question you are left holding); the audience (their program director, not a journal); and the grading rubric below, quoted in full, so the learner sees exactly how it will be judged.

THE RUBRIC, quoted in both jobs: 4 domains, each rated Level 1 (descriptive) / Level 2 (examining) / Level 3 (integrative), with one observable marker per level: description with honest detail; analysis of own reasoning; connection to prior learning or literature; forward commitment, what they will actually change.

JOB 2, THE FEEDBACK, when the educator pastes a submission after the words GRADE THIS: score each domain at its level with two sentences of evidence quoting the submission; name the single strongest sentence and why; name the one upgrade that would move the lowest domain up a level, phrased as a revision instruction the learner can act on; and end with one question that pushes the reflection deeper. Never invent quotes: if the submission is thin, score it honestly and say what is missing.

Simulation Across the Fidelity Spectrum

Simulation is not just VR headsets. The most effective simulation matches the technology to the learning goal, from text-and-audio cases to immersive VR.

A Clinician's Perspective

Immersion doesn't require a headset. A well-designed text-and-audio case, where a learner interviews a patient, orders labs, interprets an ECG, and builds a differential, can teach clinical reasoning as effectively as a $500 VR setup. Sometimes more effectively, because the cognitive load goes to reasoning, not navigating a virtual environment. What matters is the thinking the learner does, not the fidelity of the interface.

The Fidelity Spectrum

Match the tool to the goal. The most expensive simulation is not the best simulation. In our experience the text-and-audio end of the spectrum is where clinical reasoning actually gets practiced: the learner does the thinking, not the avatar. The uncanny valley effect is real. Near-human avatars and animated patients consume attention and undermine the very realism they are meant to add.

VR adds genuine value when the learning goal requires spatial interaction, physical procedure practice, or team-based immersive scenarios. For pure clinical reasoning, screen-based tools are equally effective at a fraction of the cost.

Decision Framework, Which Fidelity Do You Need?

Use text/audio when:

  • Learning goal is clinical reasoning, history-taking, or communication
  • You want unlimited repetition at near-zero cost

Use image-based/screen-based when:

  • Learning goal involves interpreting visual data (ECGs, radiology, labs)
  • You want branching decision trees with consequences

Use AR (phone-based) when:

  • Learning goal is 3D spatial understanding (anatomy, structural relationships)
  • Students have smartphones (no additional hardware)

Use immersive VR when:

  • Learning goal is procedural (physical steps, hand movements)
  • You need team-based immersive scenarios
  • High-acuity, low-occurrence event rehearsal
  • The program will use it regularly (ROI justifies hardware cost)
